# How to Write Work Instructions Operators Actually Follow

> To write work instructions, pick one task, watch an experienced operator do it, and split it into short steps of one action each. Start every step with a verb, add a photo of the critical point and state the check. Test it with a new operator. ## Why do work instructions fail?

- The instruction is a 12-page PDF nobody opens at the machine.
- Steps describe the result, not the action, so every shift does it differently.
- Photos are outdated after the last machine modification.
- New hires learn from a colleague instead, and errors show up as scrap or rework.

## What is a work instruction?

A work instruction is a document that tells one person how to perform one task, step by step. It sits below the SOP (standard operating procedure), which describes the process and who is responsible. The SOP says *what* and *who*; the work instruction says *how*, at the workstation.

## How do you write work instructions in 7 steps?

1. **Choose one task with a clear start and end.** For example "replace the filter cartridge on line 3", not "maintain line 3".
2. **Watch the best operator do it.** Film it or take photos. Note the tricks they do without thinking: that is the knowledge you want to capture.
3. **Split the task into steps of one action each.** Start with a verb: "Loosen", "Check", "Place". Keep each step under 20 words.
4. **Mark the critical steps.** Safety, quality and "point of no return" steps get a warning icon and a check, such as a torque value or a visual OK/NOK.
5. **Add one visual per step.** A photo or drawing of the exact spot beats a paragraph of text. Point to the part with an arrow.
6. **List tools, materials and PPE up front.** The operator should not have to walk away halfway through.
7. **Test with a new operator and revise.** Let someone who has never done the task follow it without help. Every question they ask is a gap in the instruction.

| Element | Must contain | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Header | Task, machine, version, owner | Filter change, line 3, v2.1, maintenance lead |
| Preparation | Tools, materials, PPE, lockout | 17 mm wrench, new cartridge, gloves, LOTO |
| Step | Verb + object + check | "Tighten the cap to 25 Nm" |
| Visual | Photo or drawing with arrow | Close-up of the cap |
| Sign-off | Who, when, deviation | Operator ID, timestamp, note |

## Work instruction example

Task: replace the filter cartridge, line 3.

1. Stop the line and apply lockout/tagout. *Check: indicator light off.*
2. Close valve V3 (quarter turn clockwise).
3. Place the drip tray under the filter housing.
4. Loosen the cap with the 17 mm wrench.
5. Remove the old cartridge and place it in the waste bin.
6. Insert the new cartridge, arrow pointing up.
7. Tighten the cap to 25 Nm. *Critical step.*
8. Open valve V3 and check for leaks for 60 seconds.

## Common mistakes

- **Writing from the office.** Instructions written without watching the task miss the real tricks.
- **Several actions per step.** "Loosen, remove and clean" becomes three steps.
- **Vague words.** "Tighten firmly" means something different to every operator. Give the value.
- **No version control.** If nobody owns the instruction, the old version stays at the machine.
- **No feedback loop.** Operators know where the instruction is wrong. Give them a way to say so.

## How do you measure success?

Measure before you change anything. Record a baseline for two to four weeks, then compare after the rollout using the same definitions. These KPIs are the most useful:

| KPI | What it tells you | How you measure it |
|---|---|---|
| Questions to the team leader | Whether the instruction is clear | Questions logged per shift or per task |
| First-time-right rate | Whether operators follow it correctly | Share of jobs without rework |
| Time to competence | Whether new hires learn from it | Days to sign-off per task |
| Instruction age | Whether content stays current | Share of instructions reviewed in the last 12 months |

Keep the list short: three KPIs that the team leader reviews every week beat ten that nobody looks at. Agree on the definitions with quality and finance up front, so the results hold up in a business case.

## FAQ

### What is the difference between an SOP and a work instruction?
An SOP describes a process, its purpose and responsibilities. A work instruction describes how one person performs one task within that process, step by step.

### How long should a work instruction be?
As short as possible while still complete. Most tasks fit in 5 to 15 steps. Split longer tasks into separate instructions.

### Who should write work instructions?
The process owner or engineer writes them together with an experienced operator. The operator brings the real-world knowledge; the writer brings structure.

### How often should you update work instructions?
After every change to the machine, product or process, and at least during each periodic review. Digital instructions make it easier because there is only one live version.
