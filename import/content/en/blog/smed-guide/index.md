# SMED: How to Cut Changeover Time Step by Step

> SMED (single-minute exchange of die) is a lean method to cut changeover time by moving as much work as possible to while the machine is still running. The goal is a changeover in single-digit minutes. Actarion makes the new standard stick: every changeover step is guided on a tablet or AR glasses, and the time per step is logged.

## Why are changeovers so slow?

- Operators search for tools, clamps and the right die after the machine has stopped.
- Every shift does the changeover in its own order, so times vary widely.
- First parts after a changeover are scrapped because settings are adjusted by feel.
- A SMED workshop delivered results, but six months later the old habits are back.

## What is SMED?

SMED is a method developed by Japanese engineer Shigeo Shingo, refined in his work with Toyota from the 1950s onward. "Single-minute" means single-digit: under ten minutes. The core idea is to separate two kinds of work:

| Type | Definition | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Internal | Can only be done with the machine stopped | Removing the old die |
| External | Can be done while the machine runs | Preheating the new die, fetching tools |

You move internal work to external, then shorten what remains. Smaller batches become affordable, and you get more capacity without new machines.

## How do you apply SMED in 6 steps?

1. **Film one complete changeover.** From the last good part to the first good part of the next product.
2. **List every activity with its time.** Mark each as internal or external.
3. **Move external activities out of the stop.** Prepare a changeover cart with all tools and parts before the machine stops.
4. **Convert internal to external.** Pre-set dies, use standard heights, preheat molds.
5. **Streamline the remaining internal work.** Quick clamps instead of bolts, fixed stops instead of measuring, parallel work by two people.
6. **Standardize and hold the gain.** Write the new sequence as a work instruction and measure every changeover.

## SMED example

An injection molding cell changes molds three times per shift.

| Activity | Before | After SMED |
|---|---|---|
| Fetch mold and tools | Internal | External: cart ready before stop |
| Preheat mold | Internal | External: preheat station |
| Disconnect water lines | Internal, one by one | Internal, multi-coupling |
| Clamp mold | Internal, bolts | Internal, quick clamps |
| Adjust settings | Trial and error | Saved recipe per mold |
| First part check | Ad hoc | Fixed check in the instruction |

The changeover time result depends on the starting point: `[CIJFER TE BEVESTIGEN]`.

## Common mistakes

- **Analysis without standard.** The workshop finds savings, but nobody writes down the new sequence.
- **Skipping step 1.** Without a film and timing you argue about opinions, not facts.
- **Only investing in hardware.** Quick clamps help, but the biggest gains are usually in organization.
- **No measurement after go-live.** Without data per changeover, drift goes unnoticed.

## How do you measure success?

Measure before you change anything. Record a baseline for two to four weeks, then compare after the rollout using the same definitions. These KPIs are the most useful:

| KPI | What it tells you | How you measure it |
|---|---|---|
| Changeover time | The core SMED result | Minutes from last good part to first good part |
| Internal vs external share | Whether prep moved off-line | Share of steps done while the machine runs |
| Start-up scrap | Whether settings are right first time | Scrap in the first minutes after changeover |
| Variation between crews | Whether the standard is followed | Spread of changeover time per shift |

Keep the list short: three KPIs that the team leader reviews every week beat ten that nobody looks at. Agree on the definitions with quality and finance up front, so the results hold up in a business case.

## FAQ

### What does SMED stand for?
Single-minute exchange of die: a changeover in single-digit minutes, under ten.

### Who invented SMED?
Shigeo Shingo developed the method, largely through his work with Toyota and other Japanese manufacturers.

### Does SMED only apply to presses?
No. It works for any changeover: injection molding, packaging lines, CNC machines and filling lines.

### How do you sustain SMED results?
By turning the new sequence into a standard work instruction, training it, and measuring every changeover.
